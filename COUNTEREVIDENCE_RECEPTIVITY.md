# Helping Participants Engage With Counterevidence

## Purpose

The project depends on participants engaging seriously with relevant facts,
including facts that may challenge an opinion they already hold. Some people
will not readily accept such evidence. Treating this as stupidity, dishonesty
or a defect of character would be both inaccurate and counterproductive.

This document considers why counterevidence can be difficult to receive and how
the project might respond. Its goal is not to make participants adopt an
editorially preferred position. It is to improve the chance that they
understand, assess and remember important evidence before deciding what they
think.

## Why resistance can be understandable

Apparent resistance to facts can have several different causes.

### Trust is part of evidence assessment

A statement does not arrive independently of its source. A participant may
have reasonable concerns about an institution's competence, incentives,
methods or earlier conduct. “An authority says so” is not always enough,
especially where institutions have made mistakes or communicated selectively.

The appropriate response is not to demand trust. It is to make the evidential
chain inspectable: what was observed, how it was measured, what the source
actually concluded, what independent checks exist and what remains uncertain.

### Opinions may be bound up with identity and belonging

Some opinions signal membership of a political, religious, professional,
national or social group. Accepting contrary evidence may feel less like
correcting a proposition and more like disloyalty, loss of status or separation
from people one values. That cost can be real even when the factual correction
is sound.

The site cannot remove that cost, but it can avoid increasing it through
ridicule, labels, triumphal language or an implication that informed people all
belong to one social camp.

### A factual disagreement may conceal a value disagreement

Many opinion propositions combine empirical predictions with moral priorities,
risk tolerances or judgements about acceptable trade-offs. Two people can agree
about the likely effects of a policy and still disagree about whether those
effects justify it.

The site must therefore avoid suggesting that a high fact score determines the
right opinion. Facts can inform a judgement without resolving every value
question within it.

### People may be protecting legitimate experience

A broad statistic can appear to contradict something a participant has seen
directly. Personal experience is not a representative sample, but it is still
evidence of something. Dismissing it can make the general evidence seem remote
or evasive.

Explanations should show how an individual case can coexist with a population
pattern, rather than treating lived experience as inherently worthless.

### The evidence itself may be incomplete or contested

Some questions have uncertain measurements, competing causal explanations or
evidence that changes over time. A participant may be resisting an unjustified
degree of certainty rather than the underlying observation.

The editorial standard should distinguish established facts, best estimates,
ranges, unresolved disputes and predictions. Uncertainty is information, not a
failure of presentation.

### The test setting can provoke defensiveness

Being told that one is incorrect can threaten competence or dignity,
particularly when the question concerns a strongly held belief. Overly easy
distractors can make the exercise look like propaganda; obscure questions can
make it look like an attempt to exclude outsiders. Patronising praise can be as
alienating as criticism.

The existing preference for concise, adult feedback and plausible wrong
answers is therefore part of the receptivity problem, not merely a matter of
style.

## The present sequencing approach

The application records each fact question's likely evidential direction. It
currently tends to begin with evidence closer to the participant's stated
position, introduces contextual material early and makes challenging evidence
more frequent as the quiz proceeds.

This approach has a reasonable hypothesis behind it: beginning with material a
participant recognises may demonstrate that the question bank understands the
stronger case for their position, reduce an immediate sense of attack and make
later challenge easier to consider.

It also creates risks:

- A participant may leave before reaching important counterevidence.
- Early supporting questions may strengthen confidence without producing the
  intended later openness.
- Hidden personalization may feel manipulative if discovered.
- Valence is an editorial judgement and may sometimes be debatable.
- Optimising order for opinion change would compromise the site's neutrality.
- Different sequences can make two participants' experiences harder to compare.

The gradual sequence should therefore remain bounded and transparent. It must
never withhold counterevidence, reduce its score, or place all of it at the end.
The current commitment to introduce challenge within the first third is
important. Completion screens and progress information should make clear that
the bank contains evidence bearing in different directions.

## Recommended factual approaches

### 1. Explain the sequencing without announcing each question's valence

The methodology should continue to state that order may respond to the
participant's view and that all participants ultimately receive the same fact
bank. The interface should not label a question “supporting” or “challenging”
before it is answered: that encourages partisan processing before the substance
has been considered.

Participants could eventually choose between:

- a gradual sequence;
- a fixed balanced sequence; and
- a randomized sequence.

Offering control may reduce suspicion while allowing the gradual approach to
remain the default if evidence supports it.

### 2. Present the strongest version of each side

A question bank should not represent one position by its strongest evidence
and the other by caricature. During whole-bank moderation, reviewers should ask:

- What would a knowledgeable advocate on each side say is missing?
- Are inconvenient but foundational facts present?
- Do explanations address the strongest plausible wrong answer?
- Would someone who disagrees recognise their actual case in the bank?

This is not a demand for equal numbers regardless of the evidence. It is a
demand for fair representation of relevant evidence.

### 3. Prefer inspectable evidence to bare appeals to authority

Where practicable, questions should concern observations or methods a
participant can understand or in principle check. An institutional source may
still be essential, but the explanation should say what the institution
measured and why the result follows.

Particularly mistrusted subjects may benefit from:

- primary data alongside an official summary;
- sources with different institutional or political incentives;
- replication or converging methods;
- a clear account of what a source does not establish; and
- direct observations, simple calculations or reproducible demonstrations.

The revised Flat Earth bank is an example of this principle: geometry,
navigation, shadows and observations from different latitudes are more useful
to a suspicious participant than repeated assertions that an authority has
settled the question.

### 4. Explain the plausible wrong answer

Feedback should not merely announce the right choice. It should explain why the
selected alternative can seem reasonable and identify the particular
assumption or distinction that makes it wrong.

This can preserve dignity while improving understanding:

> That answer would follow if the figure counted all reported incidents. The
> source counts only incidents meeting the narrower statutory definition.

Such an explanation is more useful than calling an answer a myth or
misconception.

### 5. Separate observation, inference and judgement

Where a fact bears on an opinion through several inferential steps, the
explanation should make those steps visible:

1. what was observed;
2. what conclusion the observation supports;
3. how strongly that conclusion bears on the proposition; and
4. which value judgement remains for the participant.

This reduces the impression that the site is smuggling a policy conclusion
into a factual correction.

### 6. Represent uncertainty precisely

Questions should use dates, populations, ranges and confidence qualifications
where these matter. They should be reviewed when sources or circumstances
change. “The evidence does not currently establish X” is sometimes the correct
answer and should not be rewritten as “X is false.”

### 7. Give participants a dignified route to disagreement

After reading an explanation, a participant should be able to report that the
question is inaccurate, unclear, irrelevant, biased or outdated. The reporting
route should be easy to find without interrupting every answer.

An optional private response could ask why the explanation was unconvincing:

- I do not trust the source.
- The explanation does not follow from the evidence.
- Important context is missing.
- The wording is ambiguous.
- I understand it but it does not change my view.

These responses should inform editorial review in aggregate. They must not
lower a participant's score or become a channel for moderator-to-user debate.

## Non-factual approaches

Facts are not the only means by which someone can become more open to examining
a belief. A non-factual intervention may sometimes make factual engagement
possible. Such interventions must be clearly separated from the scored quiz so
they do not masquerade as knowledge or alter opinion weight.

### Values affirmation

Before a difficult topic, a brief optional prompt could ask a participant to
identify a value important to them that is not at stake in the immediate
question—for example loyalty, fairness, independence, care, responsibility or
intellectual honesty—and recall a way they have acted consistently with it.

The purpose would be to reduce the sense that one challenged belief defines the
whole person. The site should not choose the value for them or use their answer
to target persuasion. The response need not be stored.

### Articulating what could change one's mind

Before beginning the facts, a participant could privately consider:

> What kind of evidence, if it existed, would make you less certain of your
> present view?

This encourages a falsifiable attitude without demanding an immediate
concession. An equally important follow-up is:

> Is there evidence that would make you more certain?

Asking both avoids implying that only one direction deserves examination. The
answers should be optional and private.

#### A record the participant can revisit

It may be useful to record the participant's answer and return it to them after
they have completed some or all of the fact bank. This could reveal a meaningful
difference between what someone expected to matter and what actually mattered,
without treating opinion change as the required result.

The question should not offer a loaded contrast such as:

- “Nothing, I know I'm right”; or
- “Enough evidence against it.”

The first answer caricatures confidence as arrogance, while the second signals
the answer the site regards as intellectually respectable. It would measure
willingness to select the socially approved response at least as much as
receptivity.

A more neutral prompt is:

> What, if anything, could lead you to reconsider your current view or become
> less certain of it?

The response could allow more than one selection:

- Evidence that an important factual assumption is wrong.
- Evidence that the likely consequences differ from what I expect.
- Independent confirmation from sources I consider credible.
- A convincing account of costs or risks I have underestimated.
- A different way of balancing the relevant values or rights.
- A change in the circumstances to which the question applies.
- A better practical alternative.
- Direct personal experience relevant to the issue.
- I cannot presently identify anything that would change my view.
- I do not think factual evidence alone could resolve my view.
- I am not sure.
- Prefer not to answer.

These options distinguish several positions that a binary answer would
collapse. “I do not think factual evidence alone could resolve my view” may be
entirely coherent where the disagreement is principally moral. “I cannot
presently identify anything” records a closed position without assigning a
boastful motive to it.

An optional short text field could capture an answer the categories miss, but
free text brings additional privacy, safeguarding and retention obligations.
The first implementation should use structured choices plus “Other” and should
not expose the response to moderators unless it has been anonymised and grouped
for research.

After the quiz, the site could show the original response privately and ask:

> Is that still an accurate account of what might lead you to reconsider?

The participant could retain it, revise it or delete it. A further optional
question could ask whether any fact affected their confidence, without asking
which opinion the site wanted them to adopt.

If retained, this record should:

- belong to the participant and opinion question;
- store the opinion position and knowledge weight at the time;
- distinguish before-quiz and later reflections;
- be visible and deletable by the participant;
- be removed with the account;
- have no effect on knowledge weight or question ordering;
- never appear in the public individual record; and
- be reported in aggregate only above the configured privacy threshold.

The project should evaluate whether recording the answer changes how people
respond. A private prompt may encourage useful forethought; a permanent record
may instead make a participant defend what they wrote. It would therefore be
sensible to compare a saved version with an unsaved reflection prompt before
making retention the default.

### Confidence separate from position

The five opinion choices describe direction and strength, but they do not fully
capture confidence. A participant might strongly prefer a policy while being
uncertain about its likely effects, or select a moderate position with high
confidence.

An optional confidence measure before and after the quiz could normalise
revising certainty without requiring a change of side. “I am less certain than
before” is a meaningful outcome even when the registered opinion remains the
same.

Confidence should not affect public opinion weight unless a separate,
well-justified methodology is developed and approved.

### Perspective-taking

The site could offer short, private prompts such as:

- What concern do you think leads a reasonable person to the opposing view?
- What is the strongest argument for the response furthest from yours?
- What cost is the other side most worried about?
- What part of your own position would be hardest to defend?

These are not questions with scored correct answers. Their value lies in
requiring a participant to represent another position accurately before
rejecting it.

The prompt should ask for a reasonable opposing view, not demand sympathy with
an extreme or abusive one. Responses need not be published or shared with
another user.

### Common-ground framing

An introduction may identify a genuine objective shared across positions—for
example public safety, human dignity, accountable government or reliable
evidence—before stating where disagreement begins.

This must be specific and true. Vague declarations that “we all want the same
thing” can seem patronising and may conceal real value conflicts.

### Moral and value translation

People sometimes reject an argument because it is expressed only in the moral
language of another group. An issue can be explained through more than one
legitimate concern: fairness and responsibility, liberty and protection, care
and institutional reliability.

The site may acknowledge these different considerations, but it should not
profile a participant and secretly reframe material to exploit their values.
Any alternative framing should be available to everyone and clearly presented
as framing rather than fact.

### Narratives and lived experience

A carefully selected first-person account can make consequences comprehensible
in a way that a statistic cannot. It may also help a participant understand why
another person assigns different importance to the same facts.

Narratives create serious editorial risks: vivid cases can overwhelm base
rates, authenticity can be difficult to establish, and a curated story can
become emotional manipulation. If used, they should:

- be clearly labelled as individual experience rather than representative
  evidence;
- be paired with relevant population-level context;
- include experiences bearing in more than one direction where appropriate;
- have consent and provenance; and
- never contribute to the factual knowledge score.

For the initial product, structured perspective-taking is safer than publishing
participant stories.

### Intellectual-humility prompts

Occasional neutral reminders can make uncertainty permissible:

- Reasonable people can share the facts and weigh consequences differently.
- A source can be useful without being infallible.
- Revising confidence is not the same as admitting that one's earlier view was
  foolish.
- Understanding an argument does not require agreeing with it.

These should be used sparingly. Repeated therapeutic or encouraging messages
would conflict with the site's adult tone.

### A pause before restating an opinion

Immediately after corrective feedback, a request to revise one's opinion may
feel like a demand for compliance. The site should allow participants to finish
the bank, review explanations and then revisit their opinion voluntarily.

A brief summary could ask:

- Which facts, if any, were new to you?
- Which remain uncertain or unconvincing?
- Has your position, strength of feeling or confidence changed?

The participant must remain free to answer “none” without penalty.

## A possible reflection layer

The safest way to introduce non-factual methods would be a separate optional
**Before you continue** or **Consider the other case** layer between groups of
fact questions. It would have these properties:

- no correct answer;
- no effect on knowledge weight;
- no public display;
- no user-to-user response;
- no requirement to type personal information;
- a visible skip action;
- the same prompts available regardless of opinion direction; and
- minimal or no storage unless the participant explicitly agrees to research
  use.

The first trial should use one or two structured prompts rather than free-form
psychological profiling. For example:

> Which concern best explains why an informed person might choose a different
> response: consequences, fairness, liberty, uncertainty, trust in institutions,
> or something else?

Even here, categories must not imply that disagreement is a pathology.

## Changes to editorial practice

Whole-bank review should add the following checks:

1. **Directional coverage:** Are foundational facts included even when they
   challenge the likely majority view?
2. **Source inspectability:** Does each explanation show what supports the
   answer rather than relying only on institutional status?
3. **Strong alternatives:** Does feedback answer the strongest plausible wrong
   choice fairly?
4. **Fact/value separation:** Is a moral or policy judgement being presented as
   if it were a factual answer?
5. **Uncertainty:** Does wording express the source's actual level of certainty?
6. **Dignity:** Would a knowledgeable dissenter regard the question and
   explanation as serious?
7. **Self-containment:** Can the question be understood without another item in
   the bank?
8. **Sequence robustness:** Would the question still work if encountered first?

Moderator calibration should examine the bank as a sequence as well as thirty
isolated items.

## Measuring whether an approach helps

Success must not mean movement toward the proposition, away from it, or toward
the aggregate. Appropriate measures include:

- completion of the full fact bank;
- dropout position within the sequence;
- correctness on supporting, contextual and challenging material after
  adjusting for question difficulty;
- retention when questions are reviewed later;
- use of source links;
- accurate representation of the opposing case;
- changes in expressed confidence;
- voluntary opinion revision in either direction; and
- rates and reasons for reports of bias, inaccuracy or unclear wording.

Opinion change is an outcome worth describing, not the objective to maximise.
A participant who understands the counterevidence and retains the same opinion
may be engaging exactly as the project intends.

Any experiment comparing sequences or reflection prompts should specify its
outcome measures in advance. It should not optimize covertly for persuasion,
and it should not use sensitive personal data to discover which emotional
appeal changes a particular person's view.

## Recommended implementation sequence

### Stage 1: strengthen the present factual experience

- Retain bounded gradual sequencing, with challenging evidence appearing in
  the first third.
- Audit dropout by sequence position and evidential direction in aggregate.
- Add the new whole-bank editorial checks.
- Continue removing absurd distractors and patronising feedback.
- Improve explanations of why plausible wrong answers fail.
- Make uncertainty and limits more explicit.

### Stage 2: provide transparency and control

- Explain adaptive ordering concisely before the quiz.
- Offer a fixed balanced or randomized order as an alternative.
- Show directional coverage for the completed bank without revealing valence
  before individual answers.
- Add structured, private reasons for finding an explanation unconvincing.

### Stage 3: trial a non-factual reflection layer

- Introduce one optional, symmetric perspective-taking prompt.
- Do not score or initially retain the response.
- Measure completion, later recall, confidence and reports of bias—not desired
  opinion movement.
- Review whether participants find the prompt useful, intrusive or
  patronising before adding another.

### Stage 4: carefully consider richer material

- Consider confidence tracking separate from opinion position.
- Consider a voluntary “what could change my mind?” note stored privately for
  the participant.
- Consider curated lived-experience material only after standards for consent,
  provenance, balance and statistical context have been established.

## Approaches to avoid

- Defining openness as agreement with moderators or the aggregate.
- Hiding important counterevidence until a participant is unlikely to reach it.
- Secretly targeting moral or emotional frames based on inferred identity.
- Awarding factual weight for reflection, empathy or stated willingness to
  reconsider.
- Telling participants that resistance proves bias or ignorance.
- Presenting disputed inference as settled fact.
- Using ridicule, “myth-busting” theatrics, celebration or back-slapping.
- Publishing participants' reflections or exposing them to other users.
- Allowing vivid stories to substitute for representative evidence.
- Treating all institutional distrust as irrational.
- Measuring success by how many people move in one editorially favoured
  direction.

## Recommended position

The project should continue to combine a bounded, transparent progression of
factual material with serious explanations and inspectable sources. It should
add a small optional reflection layer only after the factual experience is
strong enough to merit trust.

The most promising non-factual methods are not attempts to overwhelm resistance
with emotion. They are ways of preserving autonomy and dignity: allowing
uncertainty, separating identity from a single belief, representing the other
case accurately, clarifying what evidence could matter and distinguishing
facts from values. Used cautiously, these measures can help a participant look
at counterevidence without turning the site into a persuasion system.
