# Veganism: evidence essay pilot

## Editorial status

Unpublished pilot drafted from the 32 active Veganism fact questions on 16 August

## Canonical essay

# Veganism: what health, environmental and animal-welfare evidence can tell us

Could most people who are able to live healthily without animal products
reasonably be expected to avoid them? The question brings several arguments
together. A diet without animal products has to be nutritionally workable. Food
production affects climate, land and public health. It also affects the animals
being raised and killed. Evidence can help us understand all of those effects,
but it cannot decide by itself how much moral weight each should carry.

## A vegan diet can be healthy, but it has to be planned

The basic nutritional question is less mysterious than public debate sometimes
makes it sound. The [National Health Service's guidance on vegan
diets](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegan-diet/)
says that a varied and balanced vegan diet can provide the nutrients an adult
needs. Beans, pulses and other plant foods provide protein; cereals and pulses
also have complementary amino-acid profiles.

That does not mean that removing animal products automatically produces a
healthy diet. Some nutrients require deliberate attention. Vitamin B12 has few
vegan sources and may need to come from fortified foods or a supplement. The NHS
also highlights vitamin D, iodine, selenium and calcium. Iron is available in
plant foods, but it is generally absorbed less readily than iron from meat;
eating vitamin C at the same time assists absorption. Nor does a
"plant-based" label guarantee nutritional quality: products can still contain
large amounts of salt, free sugars or less healthy fats.

Substitutes that perform the same culinary job are not always nutritionally
equivalent. A 2025 assessment by the UK's [Scientific Advisory Committee on
Nutrition and Committee on
Toxicity](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/plant-based-drinks-health-benefits-and-risks/sacn-and-cot-assessment-of-the-health-benefits-and-risks-of-consuming-plant-based-drinks-summary)
found considerable variation among soya, oat and almond drinks. It recommended
fortification with nutrients normally supplied by cows' milk and noted that
soya generally supplies more protein than almond or oat drinks. These
differences matter particularly for young children, whose energy and nutrient
needs leave less room for poorly chosen substitutions.

The qualification in the opinion question—people who *can* live healthily
without animal products—is therefore important. Nutritional needs, medical
conditions, available food, cost and life circumstances differ. General advice
cannot establish that the same diet is practicable for every person.

## Health evidence is more complicated than a contest between two diets

Some health evidence favours reducing particular animal products. The
[International Agency for Research on
Cancer](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf)
classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans on the strength of the
evidence linking it to colorectal cancer. That classification describes the
strength of the causal evidence, not the size of the risk or equivalence with
other hazards in the same category. The [World Cancer Research
Fund](https://www.wcrf.org/preventing-cancer/topics/meat-fish-dairy-and-cancer-risk/)
advises eating little, if any, processed meat and limiting unprocessed red meat.
It does not treat red meat, fish, eggs and dairy as if their health effects were
identical.

Research comparing whole dietary groups produces a mixed picture. In the
European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition Oxford cohort,
vegetarians had a lower rate of ischaemic heart disease but a higher rate of
total stroke than meat eaters in one [published
analysis](https://www.bmj.com/content/366/bmj.l4897). A separate
[analysis of fractures](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-020-01815-3)
found higher risks of total and hip fractures among vegans. Differences in body
mass index, calcium and protein accounted for some, but not all, of the observed
fracture association.

These results deserve attention, but they do not show that choosing one diet
caused every difference. Participants were not randomly assigned to eat in a
particular way. Researchers adjusted for known differences between the groups,
but unmeasured differences may remain, and the contents of diets within the
same broad label can vary considerably. The [World Health Organization's
European review](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/WHO-EURO-2021-4007-43766-61591)
therefore describes evidence about the long-term health effects of vegetarian
and vegan diets as incomplete.

The sound conclusion is neither that all vegan diets improve health nor that a
diet without animal products is inherently unsafe. Avoiding processed meat,
eating more nutritious plant foods and planning the replacement of nutrients
are distinct choices that broad labels can conceal.

## Food choices affect climate and land, but not uniformly

Animal agriculture is large enough to matter to the climate. The [Food and
Agriculture Organization](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/new-fao-report-maps-pathways-towards-lower-livestock-emissions/en)
estimated that livestock agrifood systems produced about 6.2 billion tonnes of
carbon-dioxide equivalent in 2015, around 12% of all human-caused greenhouse-gas
emissions. One important mechanism is enteric methane: microbes ferment plant
material in the rumen of cattle and sheep, producing methane that the animals
mainly expel by belching.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change concludes that affordable,
diverse, plant-rich diets can substantially reduce emissions and bring health
co-benefits, especially in affluent settings where calories and
emissions-intensive animal products are consumed in excess. That qualification
matters. Advice aimed at overconsumption in high-income countries should not be
applied unchanged to people who cannot meet basic energy and nutrient needs.

The possible scale is illustrated by global modelling cited in the
[Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's land
chapter](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-7/). For a 2010
reference year, a scenario excluding animal-source food estimated reductions of
about 3.1 billion hectares of agricultural land and 6.5 billion tonnes of
carbon-dioxide-equivalent emissions each year. These are modelled comparisons,
not observed changes or promises that every released hectare would be restored
to nature.

The category "animal product" is also environmentally diverse. A major global
[life-cycle study in
*Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaq0216) included
land-use change, agricultural inputs, farm emissions, processing, transport,
packaging and retail. It found large differences by species, production method
and supply chain, although meat and dairy generally had greater impacts than
plant foods and ruminant meat was especially intensive. Replacing beef and
lamb can therefore have a different environmental effect from replacing every
egg, fish or dairy product.

## Scale, legal compliance and animal experience are different questions

The moral argument concerns not only how animals are killed but also how they
live. The [Food Standards Agency's annual animal-welfare
report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fsa-260608-annual-animal-welfare-report/annual-animal-welfare-report-202526)
recorded approximately 1.036 billion animals processed in UK slaughterhouses in
2025/26. It reported that 33,679—about 0.0032%—were affected by detected breaches
of welfare law at slaughter.

The small recorded proportion is evidence of high compliance with the rules
being monitored. It is not a measurement of every unpleasant experience, an
assessment of conditions throughout an animal's life or an answer to whether
slaughter is morally acceptable. Legal standards and scientific welfare advice
also need not coincide. England's rules permit enriched cages providing at
least 750 square centimetres per laying hen, while the [European Food Safety
Authority](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/plain-language-summary/welfare-pigs-farm)
has recommended farrowing pens rather than crates for sows around birth and
while nursing piglets. Such facts make conditions more concrete, but a factual
description still cannot determine what conditions a person considers
acceptable.

Animal production can also create risks beyond the farm. The [World Health
Organization's antibiotic
guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550130) recommends
stopping routine use of medically important antibiotics for growth promotion
and disease prevention in healthy food-producing animals. Practices are not
fixed, however. UK population-adjusted sales of veterinary antibiotics for
food-producing animals [fell by 57% between 2014 and
2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/veterinary-antimicrobial-resistance-and-sales-surveillance-2024/uk-varss-2024-highlights).
That demonstrates substantial reduction in sales; it does not by itself prove
an equal reduction in antimicrobial resistance.

## What the evidence cannot decide

The evidence supports several conclusions at once. Many adults can obtain the
nutrients they need without animal products, but doing so requires appropriate
foods, fortification or supplements and may be harder or unsuitable in some
circumstances. Reducing some animal products can bring health and environmental
benefits, but products and diets differ. Animal production occurs on a very
large scale, while legal compliance can be high and practices can improve.

None of this turns the word *should* into a scientific result. People may give
different moral weight to animal suffering, climate effects, health risk,
personal responsibility, cost, culture, pleasure and practical constraint.
Facts can reveal the consequences and expose weak assumptions. Deciding what
those consequences require remains a judgement.
