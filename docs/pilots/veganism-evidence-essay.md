## Canonical essay

# Veganism: what do we know, and what remains a matter of judgement?

The case for veganism is often made in a single leap: if we can live without
using animals, why wouldn't we? Yet there is quite a lot packed into that “if”.
Can people reliably remain healthy? How much environmental difference does diet
make? What happens to animals within the food system, and how far can farming
practices improve? Even once those questions have been answered, there is still
the separate matter of what any of us ought to do about it.

It helps, then, to take the evidence in stages. Some of it makes avoiding animal
products look relatively straightforward; some introduces genuine cautions and
trade-offs. Taken together, it can give us a clearer view of the choice without
pretending to make the choice for us.

## Can a vegan diet provide what people need?

For most adults, the short answer is yes—with some planning. The [National
Health Service's guidance on vegan
diets](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/how-to-eat-a-balanced-diet/the-vegan-diet/)
says that a varied and balanced vegan diet, including fortified foods or
supplements where necessary, can provide the nutrients the body needs. Protein
need not be the obstacle it is sometimes assumed to be: beans, pulses and other
plant foods are useful sources, while cereals and pulses complement one another
in the essential amino acids they provide.

Planning matters because a diet can exclude animal products without being either
varied or balanced. Vitamin B12 is the clearest example. Its vegan sources are
limited, so fortified foods or a supplement may be needed. The NHS also draws
attention to vitamin D, iodine, selenium and calcium. Plant foods can provide
iron, although the body generally absorbs it less readily than iron from meat;
having a source of vitamin C with the same meal helps with absorption.

In other words, “plant-based” and “healthy” are not synonyms. A product does not
become nutritious simply because its ingredients are vegan, and it may still be
high in salt, free sugars or less healthy fats. Equally, two products that serve
the same purpose in the kitchen may do rather different jobs nutritionally. A
2025 assessment by the UK's [Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition and
Committee on
Toxicity](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/plant-based-drinks-health-benefits-and-risks/sacn-and-cot-assessment-of-the-health-benefits-and-risks-of-consuming-plant-based-drinks-summary)
found considerable variation between soya, oat and almond drinks. It recommended
fortification with nutrients normally supplied by cows' milk and noted that
soya generally provides more protein than almond or oat alternatives.

For a healthy adult with a varied diet, those distinctions may be quite
manageable. They matter more for young children, who have less room for a
substitute that supplies too little energy, protein or particular nutrients.
They may also matter differently to someone with a medical condition, restricted
food choices or limited access to suitable fortified products. This is why the
qualification in the question—people who *can* live healthily without animal
products—is doing important work. General nutritional guidance cannot tell us
that exactly the same diet is practical for everyone.

## What do studies of health outcomes tell us?

Here the picture becomes less tidy. There are good health reasons to reduce some
animal products, but that does not mean every animal product has the same effect,
or that every vegan diet is healthier than every non-vegan one.

Processed meat provides the clearest example. The [International Agency for
Research on
Cancer](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf)
classifies it as carcinogenic to humans on the strength of the evidence linking
it to colorectal cancer. This is sometimes misunderstood: the classification
describes confidence that a causal link exists, rather than saying that all
carcinogens pose an equal risk. Reflecting the distinction between foods, the
[World Cancer Research
Fund](https://www.wcrf.org/preventing-cancer/topics/meat-fish-dairy-and-cancer-risk/)
advises eating little, if any, processed meat and limiting unprocessed red meat;
it does not extend an identical conclusion to fish, eggs and dairy products.

Studies that compare people with different overall diets are harder to sum up.
Within the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition Oxford
cohort, one [published
analysis](https://www.bmj.com/content/366/bmj.l4897) found that vegetarians had
a lower rate of ischaemic heart disease than meat eaters, but a higher rate of
total stroke. A separate [analysis of
fractures](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-020-01815-3) found
higher risks of total and hip fractures among vegans. Accounting for differences
in body mass index, calcium and protein reduced some of the fracture association,
but did not remove it entirely.

These findings should not be waved away, but neither can they tell us quite as
much as a randomised experiment. People chose their own diets, and people who
make different dietary choices may differ in other relevant ways. Researchers
can adjust for the differences they have measured, but not for every unmeasured
difference; even within the category “vegan”, the food actually eaten can vary
widely. It is therefore reasonable for the [World Health Organization's European
review](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/WHO-EURO-2021-4007-43766-61591)
to describe the evidence on long-term health effects as incomplete.

The useful lesson is not that the health evidence cancels itself out. It is that
avoiding processed meat, eating more nutritious plant foods and replacing the
nutrients formerly supplied by animal foods are related but distinct choices.
Broad dietary labels can obscure that distinction.

## How much environmental difference can food make?

Quite a substantial one, although here too the type of food and the way it is
produced matter. The [Food and Agriculture
Organization](https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/new-fao-report-maps-pathways-towards-lower-livestock-emissions/en)
estimated that livestock agrifood systems produced about 6.2 billion tonnes of
carbon-dioxide equivalent in 2015—roughly 12% of all human-caused greenhouse-gas
emissions. Part of that footprint comes from enteric methane. Microbes ferment
plant material in the rumen of cattle and sheep, producing methane which the
animals mainly release by belching.

Against that background, the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate
Change](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-7/) concludes that
affordable, diverse and plant-rich diets can reduce emissions substantially
while bringing health benefits. Its conclusion is strongest in affluent places
where people consume calories and emissions-intensive animal products in excess.
That context should not be discarded: advice intended for overconsumption in a
high-income country cannot simply be transferred to people struggling to obtain
enough energy and nutrients.

Global models give a sense of the possible scale. In one scenario cited in the
[Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's land
chapter](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-7/), a diet excluding
animal-source food was associated, against a 2010 reference year, with reductions
of about 3.1 billion hectares of agricultural land and 6.5 billion tonnes of
carbon-dioxide-equivalent emissions each year. Those are modelled comparisons,
not changes that have already been observed. Nor do they promise that every
hectare no longer needed for agriculture would automatically be restored to
nature.

It would also be misleading to imagine a single environmental footprint for
“animal products”. A large global [life-cycle study in
*Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaq0216) considered
land-use change, farm inputs and emissions, processing, transport, packaging and
retail. Impacts varied greatly with species, production method and supply chain,
although meat and dairy generally had greater effects than plant foods and
ruminant meat was particularly intensive. Giving up beef and lamb may therefore
make a different environmental contribution from giving up every egg, fish or
dairy product.

## What is life like for animals in the food system?

Any answer has to begin with scale. According to the [Food Standards Agency's
annual animal-welfare
report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fsa-260608-annual-animal-welfare-report/annual-animal-welfare-report-202526),
approximately 1.036 billion animals passed through UK slaughterhouses in 2025/26.
Of those, 33,679—about 0.0032%—were recorded as affected by detected breaches of
welfare law at slaughter.

Both figures are informative, but they answer different questions. The low
breach rate indicates high compliance with the particular rules being monitored;
it does not count every unpleasant experience, describe conditions over an
animal's whole life or settle whether slaughter itself is acceptable. Even full
legal compliance does not mean that law and current scientific welfare advice
are identical. England permits enriched cages providing at least 750 square
centimetres for each laying hen, for example, while the [European Food Safety
Authority](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/plain-language-summary/welfare-pigs-farm)
has recommended that sows around birth and while nursing piglets be kept in
farrowing pens rather than farrowing crates.

Figures and minimum areas can make otherwise abstract conditions easier to
picture, but they cannot tell a reader which conditions are morally acceptable.
They also should not leave the impression that farming practices never change.
One example concerns antibiotics, whose use in food production can contribute
to a wider public-health problem. The [World Health Organization's
guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550130) recommends
ending routine use of medically important antibiotics for growth promotion and
disease prevention in healthy animals. In the UK, population-adjusted sales of
veterinary antibiotics for food-producing animals [fell by 57% between 2014 and
2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/veterinary-antimicrobial-resistance-and-sales-surveillance-2024/uk-varss-2024-highlights).
That is a substantial reduction in sales, although it does not by itself prove
that antimicrobial resistance fell by the same amount.

## So, should people avoid animal products?

By this point, some of the factual landscape is reasonably clear. Many adults
can remain healthy without animal products, provided that they choose suitable
foods and pay attention to nutrients such as vitamin B12. The health evidence is
not uniformly favourable to either broad dietary category, but there are sound
reasons to limit particular products. Producing animals for food has substantial
effects on emissions and land, though those effects vary, and it involves very
large numbers of animals living under conditions that people may judge quite
differently. Legal compliance can be high, and production practices can improve,
without making every underlying concern disappear.

What the evidence cannot do is turn *should* into a scientific measurement. One
person may regard avoidable harm to animals as decisive; another may place more
weight on culture, affordability, health uncertainty, personal freedom or the
difference any one consumer can make. Those are not gaps that another emissions
estimate or nutritional table will necessarily close. The point of examining
the evidence is not to make values irrelevant, but to make clearer what those
values are being applied to.
