# United States power since January 2025: outcome scorecard

## Purpose and scope

This scorecard tests the empirical proposition:

> Since 20 January 2025, United States foreign policy has, on balance,
> increased the country's power to shape international outcomes in its favour,
> including when other major powers resist.

It is an editorial control for selecting and weighting evidence. It is not a
participant score, a claim that geopolitical power has a natural unit, or a
substitute for the underlying sources.

## Method

The review first identifies major episodes without regard to their direction.
An episode qualifies when it materially affects a major military objective,
alliance relationship, economic relationship, international institution or
counter-alignment involving the United States or another major power.

Each episode is assessed for:

- the outcome the United States sought;
- the outcome obtained by the review date;
- reciprocal concessions and material costs;
- durability and implementation;
- attribution to policy after 20 January 2025 rather than inherited conditions;
- evidential confidence; and
- overlap with other observations, so several measurements of one episode are
  not counted as separate outcomes.

Domain assessments use an ordinal scale from `-2` (strong net decrease in
power) through `0` (mixed or no established net change) to `+2` (strong net
increase). Half-points may record a genuinely intermediate assessment. The
scores aid consistency; the reasons and sensitivity analysis control the
conclusion.

## Major episodes

| Episode | Principal observed outcome | Direction | Attribution and durability |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| NATO investment | Allies made a 2035 five-per-cent commitment and European allies and Canada increased realised spending in 2025. | Supports | US pressure contributed, but Russia's threat and decisions predating the review period also contributed; most of the headline commitment is future capability. |
| Asian alliance capability | The Philippines agreed a broader deterrence programme with the United States. | Supports, limited | The programme is attributable to the period, but much remains planned and deterrent performance has not been tested. The Japan command conversion is excluded because it was announced in 2024. |
| China trade confrontation | China offered critical-mineral access, while the United States reduced or suspended tariffs and controls after reciprocal escalation. | Mixed | A realised bargain with a major competitor, but neither cost-free compliance nor a clear unilateral victory. Implementation remains material. |
| European Union and Japan trade agreements | The United States obtained asymmetric announced tariff, market-access and investment commitments. | Supports | Strong evidence of market leverage, qualified by US tariff costs and the difference between announced and realised investment. |
| Wider tariff effects | The tariff shock and foreign countermeasures reduced forecast global growth and produced retaliation and policy uncertainty. | Counters | Closely attributable to the new policy, although forecasts and realised long-run effects are not identical. |
| Ukraine and Russia diplomacy | The United States assembled an unusual Security Council coalition, split from European allies in the General Assembly, and made the Ukraine reconstruction fund operational. | Mixed | Demonstrates diplomatic reach and an implemented instrument, without yet establishing a durable settlement favourable to the United States. |
| Development and institutional influence | US development assistance fell sharply and withdrawal from the Paris Agreement took effect. | Counters | Directly attributable reductions in instruments and formal venues of influence; the value of freedom from institutional constraint remains a counterconsideration. |
| International reputation and allied trust | Approval and perceived reliability fell substantially, particularly in Canada. | Counters | The timing and scale are relevant, but surveys cannot isolate every policy cause or translate mechanically into government behaviour. |
| Iran war and nuclear strikes | The United States demonstrated long-range strike capacity, but the durable effect on Iran's nuclear programme was initially unresolved. | Mixed | Military capability was demonstrated; strategic success must be judged from later outcomes rather than immediate claims. |
| Strait of Hormuz | War reduced tanker traffic almost to a standstill; ceasefire and US security support produced an incomplete and fragile recovery. | Strongly counters | A realised, highly material test of whether military and diplomatic power secured a stated international outcome. Multiple Hormuz facts describe this one episode. |
| Allied conduct during the Iran war | Canada did not join the offensive; NATO did not act collectively; European bases nevertheless enabled substantial operations. | Counters, qualified | Direct evidence of incomplete mobilisation with substantial operational access. Reasons include consultation, policy disagreement, domestic constraints and wider trust. |
| Greenland dispute | Denmark, Greenland and leading European allies coordinated resistance to US pressure over sovereignty. | Counters | Direct response to post-January US policy; later effects on other alliance decisions cannot be assumed. |
| BRICS and regional counter-alignment | BRICS coordinated opposition to tariffs, while Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan created a mutual-defence framework outside direct US leadership. | Counters, limited | Demonstrates alternative organisation and resistance, but neither group is a unified anti-US bloc and the new defence pact remains untested. |

## Domain assessment

| Domain | Score | Reason |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Usable military and strategic outcomes | -1.0 | Increased or demonstrated capability was outweighed by the failure to keep Hormuz operating normally and uncertainty over durable Iranian outcomes. |
| Alliance leverage and cooperation | -1.0 | Higher allied spending and continued base access were offset by sovereignty disputes, lower trust and incomplete participation in the Iran campaign. |
| Economic bargaining power | +0.5 | The United States obtained important concessions, but paid reciprocal costs and generated retaliation and wider economic damage. |
| Institutional and diplomatic influence | -2.0 | Aid reductions, institutional withdrawal and reputation losses reduced several established instruments of influence. |
| Resistance and counter-alignment | -1.0 | Major states coordinated resistance and developed alternative arrangements, although their cohesion and durability remain uncertain. |

On equal domain weights, the mean assessment is `-0.9`. The number is not a
natural unit of power; it records that the observed balance is materially on the
countervailing side rather than close to zero.

## Sensitivity

Three deliberately different weighting schemes leave the sign unchanged:

| Weighting | Military | Alliances | Economic | Institutional | Counter-alignment | Result |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Equal domains | 20% | 20% | 20% | 20% | 20% | -0.90 |
| Hard-power emphasis | 35% | 20% | 30% | 10% | 5% | -0.65 |
| Alliance/influence emphasis | 15% | 30% | 15% | 25% | 15% | -1.20 |

A positive result requires treating economic bargaining as overwhelmingly more
important than military outcomes, alliance cooperation and institutional
influence combined, or materially revising one or more domain assessments. That
is possible as a disclosed definition of power, but it is not a natural reading
of the published proposition.

## Editorial conclusion

As of 17 August 2026, the evidence more likely than not indicates that the
proposition is false. Confidence is moderate rather than high because several
agreements are recent, the Iran conflict remains unstable, causal attribution
is incomplete and future implementation could alter the balance.

The fact bank should therefore contain more countervailing than supportive
weight without suppressing the strongest supportive evidence. Item counts must
not be treated as votes: related facts are retained when they teach distinct
knowledge, but the whole-bank review counts their shared episode once.

The scorecard must be reviewed whenever a foundational outcome changes and
again immediately before publication if the Iran war, Hormuz traffic or major
trade agreements have materially developed.
